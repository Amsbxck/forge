# Strength Training — Research-Based Loading Rules

## Purpose
Research-based parameters for **how** to load strength sessions, not which exercises to do. Weekly structure: **1× upper body** and **1× lower body + core**.
Goal: **more maximal strength, better economy and fewer injuries, with as little muscle growth as possible.**

## Strength vs muscle growth — what the studies say
Rep range alone does **not** prevent muscle growth. Sets taken to or near failure grow muscle with both heavy and light loads (Schoenfeld 2017, load). Concurrent endurance training does not block growth on average either (Schumann 2022). The variables that steer the outcome toward strength rather than size are:

| Lever | Toward strength, less growth | Toward more growth | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Load | Heavy: ~75–85 %+ 1RM | Any load, if taken near failure | Schoenfeld 2017 (load), Llanos-Lagos 2024 |
| Effort per set | Stop well before failure (low velocity loss, ~20 %) | Grinding reps, high velocity loss (~40 %) | Pareja-Blanco 2017 |
| Weekly sets per muscle | Low: < 5 sets | 10+ sets | Schoenfeld 2017 (volume) |
| Lifting speed | Maximal intended speed on the lifting part | Slow, fatigued reps | Rønnestad & Mujika 2014, Pareja-Blanco 2017 |
| Endurance load around it | High endurance volume (body mass did not rise in endurance athletes over 8–16 weeks) | Low endurance volume | Rønnestad & Mujika 2014 |

Some muscle growth is still likely: even < 5 weekly sets gave ~5 % growth (Schoenfeld 2017), and the 20 % velocity-loss group still grew ~3 % (Pareja-Blanco 2017). In endurance athletes, target-muscle growth was small (~3–6 %) and body mass stayed stable (Rønnestad & Mujika 2014).

## Loading parameters (how to load)
Values are guide values derived from the studies, not fixed prescriptions.

| Parameter | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Reps per set | **6–8** (studies used 4–10RM) | Rønnestad & Mujika 2014 (4–10RM); Androulakis-Korakakis 2021 (main lifts 1–5 reps, accessories 6–10) |
| Load | **~75–85 % 1RM**: heavy enough that 6–8 reps leave about 2–3 reps in reserve | Pareja-Blanco 2017 (70–85 % 1RM); Llanos-Lagos 2024 (≥ 80 % 1RM best for running economy; 40–79 % not significant) |
| Effort per set | Stop **2–3 reps before failure**; once rep speed clearly drops, end the set | Pareja-Blanco 2017 (20 % velocity loss > 40 %); Refalo 2023 (failure adds only a trivial extra) |
| Sets per exercise | **2–4** | Rønnestad & Mujika 2014 (2–3); Androulakis-Korakakis 2021 (3–6 hard sets per lift per week reached the minimum dose for strength) |
| Hard sets per muscle group per week | **~3–6** (low end of the growth curve) | Schoenfeld 2017 (< 5 sets: +5.4 %; 5–9: +6.6 %; 10+: +9.8 % growth); Androulakis-Korakakis 2021 |
| Rest between sets | **2–3 min** | Rønnestad & Mujika 2014 |
| Lifting tempo | Lifting part **as fast as possible**, lowering part controlled | Rønnestad & Mujika 2014; Pareja-Blanco 2017 (velocity-based) |
| Exercises — lower body + core | **2–4 lower-body** (mainly multi-joint) + **1–3 core** | Balsalobre-Fernández 2016 and Rønnestad & Mujika 2014 (2–4 lower-body exercises) |
| Exercises — upper body | **3–5** (mainly multi-joint) | No endurance-specific trial; same loading logic applied |
| Total exercises per session (guide) | **~4–6** | Derived from the above |
| Sessions per muscle group per week | **1** (upper 1×, lower 1×) | Spiering 2021 (1×/week maintains); Androulakis-Korakakis 2021 (1–3×/week builds strength in trained lifters); Rønnestad & Mujika 2014 (2×/week to build, 1×/week to maintain) |

### Rough link between reps and %1RM
- 6–8 reps with 2–3 reps in reserve ≈ a load you could lift ~8–11 times ≈ ~75–80 % 1RM.
- 6 reps with ~1–2 reps in reserve ≈ ~80–85 % 1RM.
- The relationship varies between people and exercises, so reps in reserve (or bar speed) is the better day-to-day guide than a fixed %1RM.

## Progression and phases
- **Start:** learn technique with lighter loads first; in the first 2–3 weeks of strength training, keep endurance load lower (Rønnestad & Mujika 2014).
- **Build:** when the target reps leave clearly more than 3 reps in reserve, raise the load to keep the relative intensity.
- **Duration to see effects:** strength 6–12 weeks (Androulakis-Korakakis 2021); running economy 6–24 weeks, most programmes 8–12 weeks (Llanos-Lagos 2024, Balsalobre-Fernández 2016).
- **Maintenance (Peak / race season):** 1 heavy session per week at low volume maintained strength in endurance athletes (Rønnestad & Mujika 2014). In younger adults, strength and muscle size were kept for up to 32 weeks with 1 session/week and 1 set per exercise, **as long as the load stayed high**. Intensity is the key variable (Spiering 2021).
- **Taper:** no direct study found for strength sessions in a taper. The maintenance rule (keep load heavy, cut sets) is the closest evidence.

## Timing with endurance training
| Study | Result |
|---|---|
| Schumann 2022 (43 studies) | Concurrent endurance training did **not** reduce muscle growth (SMD −0.01) or max strength (−0.06). It **did reduce explosive strength** (−0.28), mainly when both were in the same session. Not significant when ≥ 3 h apart |
| Eddens 2018 (10 studies) | Same session: strength **before** endurance gave **+6.9 %** more lower-body dynamic strength. No effect of order on muscle growth, static strength, VO2max or body fat |

- Separate strength and endurance by **≥ 3 h**. If they must be in one session, **strength first**.
- The interference effect mainly hits **explosive strength**, not max strength or size.
- These timing findings come from **lower-body** outcomes. Upper-body strength on the same day as a swim was not studied.

## Injury prevention — evidence
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Lauersen 2014 (BJSM; 25 RCTs, 26,610 participants, 3,464 injuries) | Injury-prevention programmes compared | **Strength training RR 0.315** (≈ ⅔ fewer sports injuries); proprioception training RR 0.550; **stretching RR 0.963 (no effect)**. Overuse injuries roughly halved (RR 0.527) |
| Lauersen 2018 (BJSM; 6 RCTs, 7,738 participants) | Strength training only | RR 0.338 for acute and overuse injuries. **Dose-dependent:** each 10 % increase in strength-training volume → injury risk down by > 4 percentage points. Higher volume and intensity → more protection. No injuries caused by the training |
| De Blaiser 2018 (Phys Ther Sport; 9 studies) | Core stability as a risk factor | Weaker **core strength, core proprioception and neuromuscular control** were linked to more lower-limb injuries. Core **endurance** showed mixed results. Evidence is preliminary and associative only |

## Performance (economy) — evidence
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Llanos-Lagos 2024 (Sports Med; meta-analysis) | Strength types vs running economy | **Heavy ≥ 80 % 1RM: ES −0.27** (moderate certainty), larger at higher speeds and in runners with higher VO2max. **Plyometrics: −0.31** (at ≤ 12 km/h). **Heavy + plyometrics combined: −0.43** (largest). Submaximal 40–79 % 1RM and isometric: **no significant effect**. Programmes 6–24 weeks, 1–4 sessions/week |
| Balsalobre-Fernández 2016 (5 studies, 93 highly trained runners) | Strength training vs control | Running economy improved strongly (SMD −1.42). Programmes 2–3×/week for 8–12 weeks: 2–4 lower-body exercises at 40–70 % 1RM + up to 200 jumps + 5–10 short sprints |
| Rønnestad & Mujika 2014 (review, cycling and running) | Heavy strength training in endurance athletes | Better economy and performance in both cycling and running. Mechanisms: later recruitment of less efficient fast fibres, better neuromuscular efficiency, shift from fast IIX toward fatigue-resistant IIA fibres, stiffer tendons |
| Pareja-Blanco 2017 (22 young men, 8 weeks, 2×/week, full squat 70–85 % 1RM) | Stop sets at 20 % vs 40 % velocity loss | **20 %:** squat 1RM +18.0 %, jump +9.5 %, muscle size +3.4 %, fast IIX fibres kept, ~40 % fewer reps. **40 %:** 1RM +13.4 %, jump +3.5 %, muscle size +9.0 %, IIX fibres 14.6 → 7.2 % |

### Triathlon-specific evidence
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Luckin-Baldwin et al. 2021 (RCT, 25 long-distance triathletes, 26 weeks) | Endurance + strength vs endurance only. Weeks 0–12: 8–12 reps at ~75 % 1RM; weeks 14–26: 1–6 reps at ~85 % 1RM | Max strength up at weeks 14 and 26. In a simulated triathlon (1500 m swim, 60 min bike, 20 min run): **cycling economy improved (weeks 0–14)**, **running economy improved (weeks 14–26)**. **No body-mass change.** Endurance-only group: no economy change |
| Millet et al. 2002 (15 triathletes, 14 weeks) | + 2 heavy strength sessions/week vs endurance only | Max strength up; **running economy and hopping power better** than endurance only; VO2max unchanged |
| Berryman et al. 2018 (meta-analysis, via Etxebarria 2019) | Strength training in middle- and long-distance athletes | Effective programmes: periodised heavy strength, **2–3 sets of 4–10RM**, maximal intended lifting speed, **2×/week for 12–24 weeks**; no negative effect on VO2max |
| Bonacci et al. 2011 (preliminary RCT, 8 triathletes, 8 weeks) | + plyometrics, in triathletes whose running movement was altered after cycling | Plyometrics corrected the altered run-off-bike movement (number needed to treat = 2); running economy not different |

**Frequency check:** the triathlon and endurance build trials mostly used **2 sessions per week** per muscle group. One session per muscle group per week (1× upper, 1× lower) is well supported for **maintaining** strength (Rønnestad & Mujika 2014; Spiering 2021) and built strength in trained lifters with 3–6 hard sets per lift per week (Androulakis-Korakakis 2021), but it is **less tested for building strength in endurance athletes**.

**Load check:** the 6–8 reps at ~75–85 % 1RM used here sit between the moderate (8–12 reps, ~75 %) and heavy (1–6 reps, ~85 %) phases of Luckin-Baldwin 2021 and inside the 4–10RM range of Berryman 2018 and Rønnestad & Mujika 2014.

### Upper body
- No trial was found that tests upper-body strength training for cycling or running performance. In those sports its main study-supported role is the general injury-prevention effect (Lauersen 2014/2018).
- Swimming (systematic review, 33 studies, 2022): upper-body strength (especially isometric) was **associated** with swimming performance, but whether dry-land strength training improves swimming **remains unclear**. Dry-land power training (mainly plyometrics) improved **start** performance.

## Rules derived from the studies
- Heavy loads (~75–85 %+ 1RM) with 6–8 reps; lighter loads (40–79 %) did not improve running economy.
- Stop sets 2–3 reps before failure / at low velocity loss: more strength and jump gain, less muscle growth, fast fibres kept.
- Training to failure adds only a trivial extra effect and costs more fatigue.
- Keep weekly hard sets per muscle group low (~3–6). Volume is the strongest driver of muscle growth.
- Lift the weight as fast as possible; lower it under control.
- 2–3 min rest between sets.
- 1 session per muscle group per week is enough to maintain, and likely to build, strength when the load stays heavy. Keep the load heavy even when cutting sets.
- Strength training cuts sports injuries by about two thirds; the effect grows with dose. Stretching alone showed no protective effect.
- Core: train strength and control; the evidence for core endurance is mixed.
- Separate from endurance sessions by ≥ 3 h, or put strength first if they are in the same session.
- Expect economy gains after ~8–12 weeks. Combining heavy strength with plyometrics gave the largest economy effect.
- First 2–3 weeks: technique with lighter loads and lower endurance load.
- In triathletes, heavy strength training improved both cycling and running economy without body-mass gain (Luckin-Baldwin 2021; Millet 2002).
- Plyometrics can correct altered running movement after cycling in triathletes who show it (Bonacci 2011).
- Most build trials used 2 sessions/week per muscle group; 1×/week is solid for maintenance, less tested for building.
