# Running Training — Research-Based Session & Interval Rules

## Purpose
Research-based rules to choose and structure run sessions per phase (Grundlage, Aufbau, Peak, Taper), including track sessions, double days (incl. swim) and bricks, and to adjust them with HRV. Targets are in **% of threshold speed** (primary); **heart rate is a secondary check** and can mislead during short intervals.

## Units: % of threshold speed
- **Threshold pace** = pace you can hold for ~1 hour (≈ lactate / Garmin threshold pace).
- Targets use **% of threshold *speed*** (pace in min/km runs the other way, so percentages of pace are confusing).
- Conversion: **target pace = threshold pace ÷ (percentage / 100)**. Example: threshold 4:30/km → 110 % = 4:30 ÷ 1.10 ≈ 4:05/km; 80 % = 4:30 ÷ 0.80 ≈ 5:38/km.
- Reference: in Billat's runners, threshold-like critical speed was ~86 % of vVO2max → vVO2max ≈ 112–118 % of threshold speed.

| Intensity | % threshold speed (approx.) | 3-zone model |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery | < 70 % | Low |
| Easy / long run | 70–80 % | Low |
| Steady / marathon-ish | 82–90 % | Low–middle |
| Threshold (incl. lactate-guided 2–4.5 mmol) | 95–102 % | Middle |
| 10K pace | 100–102 % | Middle–high |
| 5K pace | 104–107 % | High |
| vVO2max (≈ 3000 m pace) | 112–118 % | High |
| 1500 m pace | ~120–125 % | High |
| Strides / 200s | 120–130 % (relaxed-fast) | Speed |

All percentage conversions are approximate; adjust after test sessions.

## How running differs from cycling (short)
- **Impact:** every step loads bones, tendons and the shin. Tissue adapts more slowly than heart and lungs, so how fast volume and single-run length increase matters as much as session choice.
- **Running economy:** oxygen cost at a given pace — improved not only by running (strength training: see [[strength-training]]).
- **Interval length:** in cycling, 30/15 beat long intervals (Rønnestad). In running, reps **≥ 3 min** gave more time near VO2max than 30-s reps (Fleckenstein 2025) — don't transfer the cycling result directly.
- **Key session quality metric:** time at ≥ 90 % VO2max. In running, judge by **pace**; heart rate overstated short-interval quality (Fleckenstein 2025).

## Intensity distribution — evidence
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Esteve-Lanao 2007 | 12 sub-elite runners, ~5 months, HR-controlled zones | Low-emphasis (80/12/8 %) vs middle-emphasis (67/25/8.5 %): 10.4 km race **157 s vs 121 s faster** (significant) |
| Muñoz 2014 | 30 recreational runners, 10 wk | Polarized (77/3/20) 10K **+5.0 %** vs between-thresholds (46/35/19) +3.6 % (difference not significant; compliant runners favoured polarized, ES 1.29) |
| Casado 2022 (systematic review, 10 studies) | Highly trained & elite distance runners | Mostly **pyramidal**; marathoners pyramidal, 1500 m polarized; threshold work (tempo or threshold intervals) **≥ 1×/week**; hard day–easy day; pyramidal in preparation → polarized in competition period |
| Filipas 2022 | 60 well-trained runners, 16 wk, load matched | **Pyramidal 8 wk → polarized 8 wk** best: 5 km +1.5 %, VO2peak +3.0 %, speed at 2/4 mmol +1.7/+1.5 %; all groups improved |
| Haugen 2022 (review) | World-class distance runners | ≥ 80 % of volume easy all year; 11–14 sessions/week; 2–3 interval sessions/week + 1 long run; race-pace volume rises toward the main race |
| Stöggl 2014; Oliveira 2024 (multi-sport, see bike note) | — | Polarized slightly better for VO2max; time-trial performance about equal across distributions |
| Muñoz, Cejuela, Seiler et al. 2014 (Ironman; 9 triathletes, same 18-week plan) | HR-zone monitoring of all training vs race time | 68 % of training time in zone 1 (below 1st threshold); race itself mostly zone 2 (59 %). **More zone-1 time → faster race (r = −0.92)**; **higher % of zone-2 time → slower race (r = 0.94)**. Correlation only, small sample |
| Mujika 2014, via Etxebarria 2019 (case: world-class Olympic-distance triathlete, 1 season) | Training description | Below threshold: **85 % of run time**, 88 % bike, 74 % swim; ~16 sessions/week |
| Rivera Kofler 2025 (trained/highly trained triathletes, 13 wk) | Polarized 75/0/25 | No significant change in any time trial (200 m swim, 4/20 min bike, 6 min run) → authors: don't drop middle-zone work completely |

**Rule:** ~80 % easy volume. Pyramidal (with weekly threshold work) in Grundlage and early Aufbau, shifting toward polarized in late Aufbau and Peak. Triathlon data point the same way: a large low-intensity share; a high share of middle-zone time was linked with slower long-course racing, but a fully polarized plan without any middle-zone work did not improve triathletes either.

## Intervals & track sessions — evidence
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Seiler & Sjursen 2002 | 12 runners: 24×1, 12×2, 6×4, 4×6 min, equal time | 1-min reps reached only ~82 % VO2max vs ~92 % for 2–6 min; physiological load highest with **4-min reps** |
| Helgerud (2006 abstract of the 2007 study) | 40 men, 8 wk, work-matched | VO2max **4×4 +8.1 %, 15/15 +6.1 %** > long slow distance and threshold runs; economy improved in all groups |
| Billat 1999 | 9 distance runners | 30 s at vVO2max / 30 s at 50 % → more time at VO2max than a continuous hard run, with lower lactate |
| Billat 2001 | Middle-aged runners, **400 m track** | 15 s / 15 s alternating **90/80 % or 100/70 % vVO2max: ~14 min at VO2max**; extreme 110 % format: ~7 min. Small gap between fast and slow parts works best |
| Fleckenstein 2025 | 12 highly trained middle-distance runners | **4×3 min at 95 % vVO2max: 328 s above 90 % VO2max** vs 24×30 s at 100 %: 201 s. HR showed the opposite (545 vs 820 s above 90 % HRmax) → don't judge by HR |
| Gunnarsson & Bangsbo 2012 | 18 moderately trained runners (VO2max ~52), 7 wk, volume −54 % | **10-20-30** (3–4 × 5 min of 30 s easy / 20 s moderate / 10 s near-max, 2 min rest): **5 km −48 s, 1500 m −21 s, VO2max +4 %**, lower blood pressure and cholesterol |
| Bangsbo 2009 | 15 trained runners, 4 wk | 30-s sprints 3–4×/wk replacing endurance work: sprint and repeated-sprint performance +7 to +27 %; **10 km and VO2max unchanged** → sprints are a supplement, not a replacement |
| Rosenblat 2020 (meta, 6 trials, incl. runners) | HIIT vs sprint intervals | Long intervals (≥ 4 min) ~2 % better time-trial gains than sprint intervals |

## Double days (two runs per day) — evidence
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Casado 2023 (model description, not a trial) | "Norwegian model" in world-class runners | 3–4 **lactate-guided threshold interval** sessions/week at **2–4.5 mmol** (often as double-threshold days) + 1 VO2max session + high easy volume. Threshold work causes less fatigue than VO2max work, so more quality sessions are possible. Mechanisms are hypotheses |
| Talsnes 2024 | 14 endurance athletes, acute crossover | Same 6×10 min at threshold as **one session vs 2 × (3×10 min), 6.5 h apart**: split version had no drift in HR/lactate/RPE, lower session RPE (6.0 vs 7.0), lower post-session HR, **less fatigue and soreness next morning**. Long-term adaptations not yet tested |
| Yeo 2008 | 14 trained cyclists/triathletes, 3 wk | Twice every second day (easy + HIT 1–2 h later, low glycogen) vs daily: more muscle adaptation markers, but compromised HIT quality; **time trial +~10 % in both** |
| Marquet 2016 | 21 trained triathletes, 3 wk, same daily carbs | "Sleep low": evening intervals → no carbs overnight → easy fasted morning session: **10 km run −2.9 %** vs no change; cycling efficiency +11 %, fat mass −8.5 %. Demanding; use sparingly |
| Haugen 2022 | World-class runners | 11–14 sessions per week (doubles are standard at elite level) |
| Etxebarria, Mujika & Pyne 2019 (triathlon review) | Elite triathletes | ~16 sessions/week (only 21 full rest days in a 50-week season, Mujika 2014). How much the three sports interfere with each other is **unclear** |
| Lum, Landers & Peeling 2010 (9 well-trained triathletes) | Hard run intervals (8 × 3 min at 85–90 % vVO2peak), then **10 h later a swim** (20 × 100 m at 90 % of 1-km time-trial speed) vs passive rest | Next-day run time to exhaustion **830 vs 728 s (+14 %)**; inflammation marker (CRP) −23 % vs ±5 %; perceived recovery the same |
| Millet et al. 2002 (4 elite triathletes, 40 weeks) | Modelling training vs performance | **Swim training was unrelated to run and bike performance** (swimming is specific); run training was related to both run and bike performance |
| Tanaka 1994 (review); Mutton 1993 (12 recreational men, 5 wk) | Cross-training | Running gains transfer to cycling more than the reverse. Replacing part of the running with cycling gave the same run gains over 5 weeks in recreational men — but never more than run-specific training |

**Rules for double days:**
- Default double: **easy run in the morning + quality session in the evening** (≥ 6 h apart; the studies used 6.5–10 h).
- **Light double threshold** (Aufbau/Peak only, HRV normal): morning e.g. 5 × 6 min, evening e.g. 10 × 1000 m or 20 × 400 m, both at **95–100 % threshold speed** — controlled, not all-out.
- Splitting a long easy run into two keeps each single run shorter (see single-run spike rule below).
- Doubles add impact sessions → introduce easy doubles first; double threshold only after easy doubles are well tolerated.

**Double days with swimming (triathlon):**
- A swim can be either half of a double day: swim + run, swim + bike, or swim + strength.
- Swimming adds **no running impact** → it doesn't count toward run volume, the single-run spike rule or the run-double progression.
- A swim in the evening after a hard run session did not hurt next-day running — it improved it (Lum 2010). A swim is a good second session on a hard run or bike day.
- Swim fitness doesn't transfer to bike/run and bike/run fitness doesn't transfer to swimming (Millet 2002) → swim sessions don't replace run/bike sessions and vice versa; they are planned on top.
- No study tested a **hard swim + hard run on the same day**; treat a hard swim as a quality session when counting hard sessions per day/week (all sports add to the same total load, which HRV reflects).
- Swim + lower-body strength on the same day: no conflict expected (different muscles). Upper-body strength + swim: see [[strength-training]] (timing rules there were tested for the legs only).

## Bricks (run off the bike) — evidence
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Bernard et al. 2003 (9 well-trained triathletes) | 20 min cycling > 80 % VO2max at 60/80/100 rpm → 3000 m run | Run **7–9 % slower** than a fresh run (626–638 vs 583 s). Cadence: no effect on total run time; after 80–100 rpm the **first 500 m** were faster |
| Vercruyssen et al. 2002 (8 triathletes) | 30 min cycling at ~73 rpm (energy-optimal), ~81 rpm (free) or 90 rpm → 15 min run | Running after cycling vs fresh: oxygen cost +11.7 %, breathing +15.7 %, stride rate +7.2 %. 81–90 rpm raised cycling energy cost and caused oxygen-cost drift during the run; ~73 rpm kept it stable |
| Olcina et al. 2019 (10 trained sprint/Olympic triathletes, crossover) | 20 min bike time trial → 12 min run | **~195 m less (≈ −6 %)** in 12 min; stride length −0.1 m; step rate, ground contact and HR unchanged |
| Stewart et al. 2022 (8 club-level triathletes) | 40 km cycling vs 5 km running → 4 min run at race pace | Running mechanical efficiency **42 vs 48 %**, more anaerobic energy (16.3 vs 7.6 kJ); running economy unchanged |
| Bonacci et al. 2010 (15 moderately trained triathletes) | 15 min cycling → 30 min run | Joint angles changed in **5 of 15** (mean 3°); 1 still changed after 5 min; muscle recruitment changed in only 1 |
| Bonacci et al. 2010 (15 moderately trained triathletes) | 45 min hard cycling → run | Movement and muscle use altered in **7 of 15 (46 %)**; the change in ankle angle at foot contact explained **67 %** of the change in oxygen cost |
| Bonacci et al. 2011 (7 elite international triathletes) | 20 min easy or 50 min hard cycling → run | **No change** in muscle use, joint angles or economy (62.4 vs 62.1; 63.4 vs 63.3 ml/kg/min) |
| Bonacci et al. 2011 (preliminary RCT, 8 affected triathletes, 8 wk) | Endurance + plyometrics vs endurance only | Plyometrics corrected the altered run-after-bike movement (number needed to treat = 2); economy not different |
| ISBS 2010 (12 subjects) | Brick workouts vs single-sport training | Bricks improved how quickly knee movement adapted from cycling to running. Small, conference abstract only |
| Constant vs variable cycling (2007, 10 triathletes) | 20 km cycling: constant, self-paced, or variable 68–92 % of max aerobic power (same mean) → 5 km run | Run fastest after **constant** power (1118 s) vs self-paced (1134 s) vs variable (1168 s) |
| Hill & St Clair Gibson 2012 (11 male triathletes) | 30 min cycling, power ± 15 % every 1 or every 5 min (same mean) → 5 km run | No difference in run time — small swings don't matter |
| Cycling intensity study 2024 (38 triathletes) | 20 km at 80, 85 or 90 % FTP → 5 km run | Men ran 35 s faster after 80 % than after 90 % FTP; women no difference. Total bike + run fastest at 90 % FTP — **sprint distance only**, no long-course trial found |

**What the brick studies show:**
- Running after cycling is slower and less efficient than fresh running (≈ 6–9 % in the short tests), even with the same heart rate and effort.
- The changes concentrate in the **first minutes** of the run (first 500 m; one athlete still altered after 5 min).
- **Moderately trained triathletes are affected far more than elite ones** (46 % vs none). Brick practice improved the transition in one small study; plyometrics corrected altered movement in another.
- Bike pacing before the run: **steady power** beats large power swings; small swings (± 15 %) made no difference. Cadence findings are mixed (lower ~73 rpm lowered energy cost in one study; no effect on run time in another).
- **Evidence gap:** no study defines the best brick frequency, length or multi-transition format. Brick placement below follows the specificity principle (race-specific work rises toward the main race: Haugen 2022, Etxebarria 2019).

**Rules for bricks:**
- The run part counts as a run: the **single-run spike rule** (≤ 10 % longer than the longest run in the last 30 days) and weekly run progression apply to it.
- Judge the brick run against **run-off-bike pace**, not fresh-run pace (expect it to be slower at the same effort).
- The key part to practise is the **first 5–10 min** off the bike → short run-offs often are useful; multi-transition bricks repeat that part several times (no trial, rationale only).
- Ride the bike part of race-pace bricks at **steady power** (no large surges).
- Bricks count toward the HRV-gated quality session count when the bike or run part is at race intensity or harder.

## Injury and form — evidence
| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Frandsen 2025 (5,205 recreational runners, 18 months, 588,000 runs) | Each run vs **longest run in the past 30 days** | Injury rate: > 10–30 % longer **+64 %**, > 30–100 % **+52 %**, > 100 % **+128 %**. Weekly distance change and acute:chronic ratio showed **no clear association**. To be confirmed by further studies |
| Nielsen 2014 (874 novice runners, 1 year) | Weekly distance progression | > 30 % increase over 2 weeks: hazard ratio 1.59 for distance-related injuries (borderline, p = 0.07) |
| Heiderscheit 2011 | Step-rate manipulation | Small step-rate increases (~5–10 % above preferred) reduced energy absorption at knee and hip |

**Rules:**
- **No single run > 10 % longer than the longest run in the last 30 days** (Frandsen 2025).
- Weekly increases well below 30 % per 2 weeks (Nielsen 2014).
- A slightly higher step rate (+5–10 %) is an option to reduce joint loading.

## HRV-guided adjustment
### Evidence
- **Kiviniemi 2007** (26 moderately fit men, 4 wk): hard session only if HRV stable or rising; easy/rest if HRV dropped below the 10-day mean − 1 SD or fell 2 days in a row. HRV group: VO2peak 56 → 60 ml/kg/min, max running speed +0.9 vs +0.5 km/h (predefined plan).
- **Vesterinen 2016** (40 recreational runners, 8 wk): 7-day rolling RMSSD vs personal band (mean ± 0.5 SD). HRV group did **fewer hard sessions (13.2 vs 17.7)**, 3000 m **+2.1 %** vs +1.1 % (n.s.); VO2max improved in both. HRV mainly helps to **time** hard sessions.
- Same method as in the bike note (Javaloyes 2019; Plews 2014: ≥ 3 valid measurements per week).
- **Plews 2012** (case comparison, 2 elite triathletes, 77 days, ~23 h/week): in the athlete who became non-functionally overreached, the 7-day rolling lnRMSSD **fell steadily** toward the key race and its day-to-day **variation (CV) also fell**; the well-adapted athlete stayed stable. A falling trend over weeks is a warning sign even before the band is crossed (2 athletes only).
- **Aubry 2014** (33 trained triathletes): athletes who were only acutely tired after a 3-week overload gained most in the taper; those who became **functionally overreached** gained less and had far more infections (70 % vs 20 % vs 10 %).
- In triathlon, HRV reflects the **total load of all sports** — hard swim, bike, run and brick sessions all count.

### Calculation (same as bike)
```
ln_rmssd[d]   = ln(nightly rMSSD)
baseline      = last 30–60 days of ln_rmssd (min 28 days to start)
band          = mean(baseline) ± 0.5 × SD(baseline)
roll7         = mean(ln_rmssd over last 7 days)          # needs ≥ 3 valid days
status        = below  if roll7 < lower band
                normal if inside band
                above  if roll7 > upper band              # treated as normal
cv7           = SD / mean of ln_rmssd over the last 7 days
warning       = roll7 lower than the week before for ≥ 2–3 weeks in a row
                (stronger if cv7 also falls)              # Plews 2012, case study
```

### Weekly rule (planning)
| roll7 status | Week plan |
|---|---|
| normal / above | Phase default number of quality sessions (all sports, incl. bricks); double days allowed |
| below | 1 quality session max, no double threshold; recovery week if also below last week |
| normal, but warning (falling trend) | Phase default, no extra overload block; recheck next week |
| < 3 valid days | Phase default, mark week "unverified" |

### Daily rule (morning)
| Today's roll7 | Action |
|---|---|
| normal / above | Train as planned |
| below, today = quality / track | **Move** it to the next easy day (≥ 48 h spacing) or **swap** for an easy run of the same duration |
| below, today = double day | Drop the second session; keep the morning run easy. A swim can stay if kept easy (no impact; a swim after hard running aided next-day running in Lum 2010) |
| below, today = brick | Do only the bike part easy, or swap for an easy single session |
| below 2+ days in a row | Easy or rest; drop one quality session from the week |

Don't slow down track/VO2max reps to "fit" a low HRV day — swap or move instead.

## Interval selection logic
1. **Goal = VO2max:** reps ≥ 3 min (1000–1200 m, 4×4) — most time near VO2max in runners. 15/15 or 30/30 as variety, only with a small speed gap.
2. **Goal = threshold / half-marathon & long-course racing:** threshold intervals or tempo ≥ 1×/week; lactate-guided 2–4.5 mmol pace (~95–102 %).
3. **Time-limited weeks:** 10-20-30 (performance gains with ~50 % less volume).
4. **Speed / economy:** strides 1–2×/week; sprints only as a supplement. Strength and plyometrics: see [[strength-training]].
5. Judge interval quality by pace, not by HR.
6. **Triathlon:** run-off-bike practice through bricks; judge the brick run against run-off-bike pace, not fresh pace.

## Phase rules
| Phase | Structure | Quality sessions/wk | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grundlage** | ~80 %+ easy, **pyramidal** | 1 (threshold) | Strides 1–2×/wk; long run grows ≤ 10 % above the longest run in the last 30 days; easy doubles possible (short morning jog). Bricks: short easy run-offs after rides. |
| **Aufbau** | ~80/20, pyramidal → polarized in the second half | 2 (1 threshold + 1 track VO2max) | Track: 1000s/1200s/4×4, 15/15 or 30/30 as variety. Optional doubles: easy AM + quality PM; light double threshold only with normal HRV and after easy doubles are tolerated. Bricks: race-pace or multi-transition bricks. |
| **Peak** | **Polarized** + race-specific | 2 | Race-pace sessions and race-specific bricks (steady bike power → race-pace run); track reps ≥ 3 min; 10-20-30 as a time-efficient option; doubles only if well tolerated. |
| **Taper** | Volume down, intensity kept | 1–2 (shortened) | 7–14 days (world-class runners 7–10; meta-analyses 8–21). Volume −41–60 % progressive, keep intensity and frequency (Bosquet 2007; 2023 meta). Overload before the taper only helps if it doesn't tip into functional overreaching (Aubry 2014, triathletes). |

Hard day – easy day rhythm throughout; ≥ 48 h between quality sessions (double-threshold days count as one quality day; bricks at race intensity or harder count as one quality session; hard swims count toward the day's and week's total).

## Rules derived from the studies
- ~80 % of running volume easy; pyramidal early, polarized late (Filipas 2022, Casado 2022).
- Threshold work at least weekly; VO2max reps ≥ 3 min preferred over 30-s reps.
- Judge interval quality by pace; HR misleads in short intervals.
- No single run > 10 % above the longest run of the last 30 days; weekly increases well under 30 % per 2 weeks.
- Double days: easy AM + quality PM first; double threshold only in Aufbau/Peak with normal HRV.
- "Sleep low" (evening intervals → no carbs overnight → easy fasted morning run) is possible but demanding; use sparingly and not in key race weeks.
- HRV below band: move or swap quality sessions, drop the second session of double days — never just run reps slower.
- Taper: cut volume, keep intensity and frequency; a pre-taper overload is useful only if it doesn't cause functional overreaching (Aubry 2014).
- Triathlon intensity: large low-intensity share in all sports; don't let middle-zone time take over long-course preparation (Muñoz 2014), but don't remove it completely (Rivera Kofler 2025).
- Swim can be either half of a double day; it adds no running impact and neither replaces nor is replaced by run/bike sessions (Millet 2002; Lum 2010).
- Bricks: the run counts toward the single-run spike rule; practise the first 5–10 min off the bike; ride the bike part at steady power; moderately trained triathletes benefit most from practising the transition (Bonacci 2010/2011).
- HRV in triathlon reflects the load of all sports; a falling 7-day trend over several weeks is a warning sign even inside the band (Plews 2012).
